# Robotics and toys on a Raspberry Pi 5: research notes

Gathered 7 November 2026, 20:24 onwards (IST), as the second half of the edge-AI research for the soft toy Hoja (a Pi 5 inside a soft toy that darkens when it hears gaalis) and for the Pi as the compute budget for small models in other people's games and robots. The first half, `research-notes.md` in this folder, covers the Pi 5 as an AI machine (thermals, power draw, storage, OS, runtimes, accelerators, audio models); none of that is repeated here, and where a point depends on it these notes say so. The rules are the same: every number carries its source and the conditions it was measured under; my own arithmetic says "derived"; where I looked and found nothing it says "not found"; prices and software versions carry the date they were read.

Package versions, read from the Raspberry Pi archive's Trixie arm64 list on 7 November 2026 (<http://archive.raspberrypi.com/debian/dists/trixie/main/binary-arm64/Packages.gz>): `python3-gpiozero` 2.0.1, `python3-lgpio` 0.2.2, `python3-rpi-lgpio` 0.6, `python3-picamera2` 0.3.38, `rpicam-apps` and `imx500-all` 1.14.0; Debian Trixie carries `python3-libgpiod` 2.2.1, the version 2 API (<https://packages.debian.org/trixie/python3-libgpiod>). Debian's own `python3-gpiozero` is the older 1.6.2 (<https://packages.debian.org/trixie/python3-gpiozero>); only 2.x defaults to lgpio, so a gpiozero from outside the Raspberry Pi archive fails on a Pi 5.

## 1. Talking to hardware from a Pi 5 on Trixie

### RP1 and what changed from the Pi 4

On the Pi 5 the 40-pin header is not driven by the main processor. RP1, Raspberry Pi's own I/O chip, sits on a PCIe 2.0 x4 link to the BCM2712 and handles USB, Ethernet, the camera and display interfaces, and the low-speed peripherals: SPI, UART, I2C, PWM, GPIO and I2S; it also carries two Cortex-M3 cores, an 8-channel DMA controller and a 12-bit ADC with a temperature sensor (documentation source, <https://github.com/raspberrypi/documentation/blob/master/documentation/asciidoc/computers/io-controllers/rp1.adoc>). The RP1 peripherals datasheet lists 28 header GPIOs in one bank with 5 UARTs, 6 SPI, 4 I2C, 2 I2S and a 4-channel PWM output among their functions (<https://pip-assets.raspberrypi.com/categories/892-raspberry-pi-5/documents/RP-008370-DS-1-rp1-peripherals.pdf>).

The practical consequence is that any library which mapped the old Broadcom GPIO registers through `/dev/mem` reaches nothing on a Pi 5. A 2026 explainer puts it plainly: "the GPIO registers are not at the main processor but at the RP1 – so RPi.GPIO reaches into empty space" (<https://raspberry.tips/en/raspberrypi-tutorials/raspberry-pi-5-gpio-rpigpio-not-working-alternatives>). The supported route is the kernel's GPIO character device (`/dev/gpiochip*`). On current kernels the header's controller is `/dev/gpiochip0`, labelled `pinctrl-rp1`; images with kernels older than 6.6.45 had it at `gpiochip4` (same page; the gpiozero changelog for 2.0.1.post3, 27 July 2026, records fixing `LGPIOFactory` "defaulting to the wrong GPIO chip on the Raspberry Pi 5, which broke on kernels that renumbered the chip from gpiochip4 to gpiochip0", <https://gpiozero.readthedocs.io/en/stable/changelog.html>). So code that hard-codes chip 4 breaks on current Trixie; `gpiodetect` shows which chip is which.

### Which GPIO libraries work, and which do not

gpiozero's own documentation now says, in its pin-factory section, "Only lgpio works on the Pi 5", and its table marks the `rpigpio`, `pigpio` and `native` factories "(not Pi 5)" (<https://gpiozero.readthedocs.io/en/stable/api_pins.html>). gpiozero 2.0 made `LGPIOFactory` the default and added Pi 5 board data (changelog, above), so `from gpiozero import LED` works unchanged on Trixie with the Raspberry Pi archive's package.

lgpio is the C library and Python module underneath. rpi-lgpio provides the `RPi.GPIO` API on top of lgpio so that old code runs, with documented differences (<https://rpi-lgpio.readthedocs.io/en/release-0.4/differences.html>); because it goes through the kernel it cannot claim a pin another driver holds, so with SPI enabled GPIO 7 and 8 report "GPIO busy", and it cannot be installed beside the original `RPi.GPIO` (<https://raspberry.tips/en/raspberrypi-tutorials/raspberry-pi-5-gpio-rpigpio-not-working-alternatives>).

libgpiod's Python bindings (`gpiod`, version 2 on Trixie) talk only to the character device and so work on every model; Adafruit measured libgpiod on the Pi 5 at "about 3 times faster than the recommended gpiozero" but moved Blinka to rpi-lgpio because libgpiod "lacked some crucial features such as PWM" (<https://learn.adafruit.com/circuitpython-on-raspberrypi-linux/using-neopixels-on-the-pi-5.md>). `pinctrl` (in `raspi-utils`) reads and sets pin functions on RP1 from the command line.

The two that do not work: the original RPi.GPIO raises "This module can only be run on a Raspberry Pi!" on a Pi 5 (<https://raspberry.tips/en/raspberrypi-tutorials/raspberry-pi-5-gpio-rpigpio-not-working-alternatives>), although Debian Trixie still ships `python3-rpi.gpio` 0.7.1~a4 (<https://packages.debian.org/trixie/python3-rpi.gpio>), so it can be installed and fail. pigpio is "hard-coded to not run on a Pi5", its daemon aborting with "Sorry, this system does not appear to be a raspberry pi"; a maintainer wrote "pi5 is not supported by pigpio. pi5 is built with a new chip architecture that is incompatible with previous iterations", and the issue was still open when read (<https://github.com/joan2937/pigpio/issues/589>). This matters for servos because pigpio's DMA-timed pulses were the old fix for servo jitter; gpiozero removed its "use pigpio" servo warning in 2.0.1.post3 (changelog, above).

One more boot-time tool worth knowing: `config.txt` accepts a `gpio=` directive that sets pins to inputs or outputs, drives them high or low and sets pulls at boot, for example `gpio=12=op,dh`; it does not touch the kernel, and `pinctrl` or Device Tree can override it later (<https://github.com/raspberrypi/documentation/blob/master/documentation/asciidoc/computers/config_txt/gpio.adoc>). Following a power-on reset, GPIO pins are generally inputs with their default pulls applied (<https://github.com/raspberrypi/documentation/blob/master/documentation/asciidoc/computers/raspberry-pi/gpio-on-raspberry-pi.adoc>). For a toy that drives a heater or a motor, what each output pin does between power-on and the program starting, and after the program crashes, is decided by these defaults and by whatever pull resistor sits on the driver's input.

### Hardware PWM on the Pi 5

RP1's PWM block has four channels on the header instead of the Pi 4's two: GPIO 12 is PWM0_CHAN0, GPIO 13 PWM0_CHAN1, GPIO 18 PWM0_CHAN2, GPIO 19 PWM0_CHAN3 (pinout.xyz, <https://pinout.xyz/pinout/pwm>; Pimoroni's Phil Howard's table, which adds GPIO 14 and 15 as alternative pins for channels 2 and 3, <https://gist.github.com/Gadgetoid/b92ad3db06ff8c264eef2abf0e09d569>). They are enabled with the old overlays: `dtoverlay=pwm-2chan` (GPIO 18 and 19 by default) or `dtoverlay=pwm-2chan,pin=12,func=4,pin2=13,func2=4`; the old `func` numbers "still work on Pi 5, where the RP1 GPIO driver maps each one to the closest matching RP1 function" (pinout.xyz, above). Two lines in `config.txt` give all four: `dtoverlay=pwm-2chan,pin=12,func=4,pin2=13,func2=4` and `dtoverlay=pwm-2chan,pin=18,func=2,pin2=19,func2=2`, checked with `pinctrl get 12,13,18,19` (raspiCamSrv documentation, <https://signag.github.io/raspi-cam-srv/latest/gpioDevices/ServoPWM/>). Control is through sysfs, and the chip number differs between reports (`pwmchip2`, <https://raspberrypi.stackexchange.com/questions/148795/only-1-pwm-pin-working-when-controlling-via-sysfs>; `pwmchip0` under Trixie, <https://github.com/berndporr/rpi_pwm>), so it has to be read on his board. `rpi-hardware-pwm` wraps the channel mapping in Python (<https://github.com/Pioreactor/rpi_hardware_pwm>). GPIO 18 and 19 are also the I2S clock and frame lines, so with an I2S microphone or audio HAT the pair to use is 12 and 13 (pinout.xyz, above). That collision matters for Hoja if its microphone is I2S rather than USB.

There is also `dtoverlay=pwm-pio,gpio=N`, a kernel driver that generates PWM from RP1's PIO block on any of GPIO 0–27, up to four instances, limited by RP1's four PIO state machines (<https://www.raspberrypi.com/news/piolib-a-userspace-library-for-pio-control/>; merged as raspberrypi/linux PR 6470, 15 November 2024, <https://github.com/raspberrypi/linux/pull/6470>).

Software PWM jitter has one careful measurement covering the Pi 5. A 2026 Jönköping University thesis captured CPU-generated software PWM and a PCA9685 hardware PWM chip simultaneously with a Saleae Logic 8 at 25 MS/s for 60 s per run, at 25, 50, 75 and 100 % CPU load, on a Pi 3B, Pi 400 and Pi 5, with a 20 ms period and a 10 ms target pulse (Elmquist and Strandman, <https://hj.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:2076182/FULLTEXT01.pdf>). On the Pi 5, software PWM pulse width had a standard deviation of 0.385 µs and a peak-to-peak spread of 10.3 µs at 25 % load, but 5.28 µs and 238.7 µs at 75 % load and 1.27 µs and 39.7 µs at 100 %; the P99–P1 spread stayed between 1.55 and 5.88 µs, so the damage is rare large outliers, not general broadening. The PCA9685 held about 1 µs standard deviation and 6.8–8.0 µs peak-to-peak on every platform and load, with a constant offset of about +33 to +40 µs from its internal oscillator. On the Pi 400 at 100 % load the software outliers reached 3,504.9 µs. Derived, assuming a hobby servo maps a 1,000 µs change in pulse width onto roughly 180°: a single 238.7 µs outlier would be a command error of about 43° for that one pulse. The thesis used 10 ms pulses rather than servo-shaped 1–2 ms ones and does not name the software PWM library in the excerpt read, so the servo-scale error on his Pi is a bench measurement.

### I2C, SPI, UART

I2C: GPIO 2 and 3 carry SDA and SCL with 1.8 kΩ on-board pull-ups to 3.3 V (<https://github.com/raspberrypi/documentation/blob/master/documentation/asciidoc/computers/raspberry-pi/gpio-on-raspberry-pi.adoc>). RP1's I2C is a Synopsys DesignWare controller; a Raspberry Pi engineer wrote that it "supports clock stretching correctly", unlike the Broadcom controller on earlier Pis (<https://github.com/raspberrypi/linux/issues/4884>). That removes a known problem for the BNO055 and BNO085 IMUs, which on a Pi 4 needed software I2C to read reliably (same issue; and a ROS driver's README, "Upgrade to Raspberry Pi 5", <https://github.com/dheera/ros-imu-bno055>). Early Pi 5 kernels had I2C compatibility problems with some devices, fixed in April 2024 (<https://github.com/raspberrypi/linux/pull/6091>).

SPI: RP1 has nine SSI controllers, six routed to the header; the Pi 5 uses the DesignWare `spi_dw` driver, DDR is not supported, and "as a rule of thumb, anything over 50 MHz is unlikely to work" (<https://github.com/raspberrypi/documentation/blob/master/documentation/asciidoc/computers/raspberry-pi/spi-bus-on-raspberry-pi.adoc>).

UART: the Pi 5 has no mini UART; its "primary" UART, `/dev/serial0`, points to `/dev/ttyAMA10` on the separate 3-pin debug connector, and the UART on GPIO 14 and 15 is a PL011 at `/dev/ttyAMA0`, off by default (<https://github.com/raspberrypi/documentation/blob/master/documentation/asciidoc/computers/configuration/uart.adoc>). A Raspberry Pi engineer: "if you want to use the UART on the 40-pin GPIO header (pins 8 & 10) on a Pi 5 then you do need to add `dtparam=uart0` to config.txt" (<https://github.com/raspberrypi/documentation/issues/3239>). Code copied from Pi 4 tutorials that opens `/dev/serial0` to talk to a microcontroller on pins 8 and 10 will open the debug connector instead. All UARTs are 3.3 V only (uart.adoc, above).

### 3.3 V logic and current limits

GPIO pins "operate at 3.3 V and aren't 5 V tolerant"; "an individual GPIO pin can supply up to 16 mA to an attached device, and all GPIO pins combined can supply a maximum total current of 50 mA" (<https://github.com/raspberrypi/documentation/blob/master/documentation/asciidoc/computers/raspberry-pi/gpio-on-raspberry-pi.adoc>; the same 50 mA combined and 16 mA per pin appear in the power-supply table, <https://github.com/raspberrypi/documentation/blob/master/documentation/asciidoc/computers/raspberry-pi/power-supplies.adoc>). The pad-control page adds that drive strength "is not a current limit": the pad is a voltage source, and a pin shorted to ground "in due time it will fail" (<https://github.com/raspberrypi/documentation/blob/master/documentation/asciidoc/computers/raspberry-pi/gpio-pad-controls.adoc>; its register tables describe the older Broadcom pads). So a pin drives the gate of a MOSFET, the input of a motor driver or the data line of an LED chain, and powers nothing.

## 2. LEDs: WS2812B and NeoPixels from a Pi 5

### Three working routes, one dead one

The old route, `rpi_ws281x`, used the Broadcom PWM and DMA registers and stopped working on the Pi 5. Its author published an experimental Pi 5 branch on 17 November 2024 that needs a kernel module (`rp1_ws281x_pwm.ko`), kernel headers, a device-tree overlay and `pinctrl`; "not all features (all channels, inverted signal, etc.) are working yet", and the usable pins are GPIO 12, 13, 14, 15, 18 and 19 (<https://github.com/jgarff/rpi_ws281x/wiki/Raspberry-Pi-5-Support>). A survey of LED drivers summarises it as "one channel working, concurrent channels not" (<https://moonmodules.org/MoonLight/design/leddriver.html>).

The SPI route encodes each WS2812 bit as several SPI bits on MOSI. Adafruit's `adafruit-circuitpython-neopixel-spi` was for a time "the only option for folks to use NeoPixels" on the Pi 5, at the cost of occupying the SPI port (<https://learn.adafruit.com/circuitpython-on-raspberrypi-linux/using-neopixels-on-the-pi-5.md>); the `rpi5-ws2812` package takes the same approach at 2.4 MHz (<https://github.com/niklasr22/rpi5-ws2812>, as described at <https://moonmodules.org/MoonLight/design/leddriver.html>).

The PIO route uses RP1's programmable I/O block. RP1 has one PIO block "almost identical to the two that RP2040 has", with four state machines and 32 instruction slots; because its registers are not reachable over PCIe, the RP1 firmware exposes a mailbox and the kernel's `rp1-pio` driver gives user space an `ioctl()` interface, wrapped by `piolib`, a clone of the Pico SDK's PIO API (<https://www.raspberrypi.com/news/piolib-a-userspace-library-for-pio-control/>; <https://github.com/raspberrypi/utils/tree/master/piolib>). WS2812 output is one of piolib's own examples, and the announcement shows "two strings of WS2812 LEDs being driven from a Raspberry Pi 5". Adafruit built `Adafruit-Blinka-Raspberry-Pi5-Neopixel` on it, which "can use any pin because PIO is not limited by underlying peripherals" (<https://blog.adafruit.com/2024/12/10/raspberry-pi-5-rp1-chip-support-for-neopixels-with-python-bindings-coming-soon/>); install is `pip install Adafruit-Blinka-Raspberry-Pi5-Neopixel`, the device `/dev/pio0` must exist (a firmware and kernel recent enough), and if it is owned by root a udev rule `SUBSYSTEM=="*-pio", GROUP="gpio", MODE="0660"` is needed (<https://github.com/adafruit/Adafruit_Blinka_Raspberry_Pi5_Neopixel>; Adafruit's guide, above, where the example drives pin D13). The package is still a release candidate, 1.0.0rc2 of 23 January 2025 (<https://pypi.org/project/Adafruit-Blinka-Raspberry-Pi5-Neopixel/>). piolib's known issue: "Blocking operations block the whole RP1 firmware interface until they complete" (piolib README, above).

### Level shifting from 3.3 V to 5 V

Adafruit's wiring guide: the data line "needs to be at least 0.7 * VDD (5 volts), or about 3.5 volts", so 3.3 V may work without conversion but "it's not really guaranteed"; the two fixes are a 74AHCT125 level shifter, or a 1N4001 diode in the 5 V feed to drop the pixels' supply to about 4.3 V so that 3.3 V counts as high, the diode limiting the chain to about 1 A ("about 16 NeoPixels at full 100% bright white") (<https://learn.adafruit.com/neopixels-on-raspberry-pi/raspberry-pi-wiring>). Thresholds differ by variant: the WS2812B-2020 datasheet (V1.3) gives a minimum high input of 2.7 V at VDD = 5 V, which 3.3 V clears (<https://cdn-shop.adafruit.com/product-files/4684/4684_WS2812B-2020_V1.3_EN.pdf>). Which chip is on a given strip or ring bought in India is not printed on the listing, so whether his parts need a shifter is a bench question. The cheap bidirectional MOSFET modules sold as "level converters" (BSS138 or 2N7002 with 10 kΩ pull-ups) are rated for "I2C, TTL serial, and slow SPI (<2MHz)" (<https://robocraze.com/products/7semi-bss138-4-ch-i2c-bi-directional-logic-level-converter-breakout>; <https://adiy.in/shop/4-channel-i2c-safe-bi-directional-logic-level-converter-2n7002/>); whether their rise time is clean enough for WS2812's 800 kHz signal was not found measured.

### Current per LED and power budgeting

Datasheet-style figures say about 50 mA per pixel at full white (Pololu's WS2812B strip page, <https://www.pololu.com/product/2550>; Adafruit allows "up to 60mA", <https://learn.adafruit.com/neopixels-on-raspberry-pi/raspberry-pi-wiring>). Paul Stoffregen measured two products both sold as WS2812B: one drew about 33.5 mA per LED at full white, the other about 52.5 mA, and both drew "just under 1 mA per LED when just sitting there dark" (<https://www.pjrc.com/how-much-current-do-ws2812-neopixel-leds-really-use/>). The WS2812B-2020 datasheet gives quiescent current under 0.6 mA (datasheet, above); a LowPowerLab bench measurement of one WS2812B gave 0.4–0.5 mA "ALL OFF" at 2.6–3.3 V (<https://lowpowerlab.com/forum/general-topics/ws2812b-rgb-led-measurements>). Derived: a 16-LED ring at full white draws 16 × 33.5–52.5 mA, about 0.54–0.84 A at 5 V (2.7–4.2 W), and 16 mA or so when dark; a toy that darkens by turning LEDs down therefore has its LED budget set by its brightest resting state, and the dark state still costs on the order of 6–16 mA for 16 pixels (derived from the 0.4–1 mA per-pixel figures). The Pi itself draws more than that at idle (2.4–3.3 W measured, in `research-notes.md` §1).

Adafruit's wiring guide allows powering "a few pixels" from the Pi's 5 V pin but routes anything more to a separate supply, with grounds joined (guide, above). The Pi 5's 5 V header pins are connected to the board's 5 V input rail and can also accept power (gpio documentation, above); how much current they can source on a Pi 5 is not stated as a figure in the documentation read.

## 3. Motors, servos, pumps and heaters

### Hobby servos

A hobby servo needs a pulse of roughly 1–2 ms repeated at about 50 Hz; on the Pi 5 the clean ways to make it are the four RP1 hardware PWM channels (§1), `pwm-pio` on any pin (§1), a PCA9685 over I2C (measured above at about 1 µs standard deviation regardless of host load), or a microcontroller (§5). gpiozero's `Servo` and `AngularServo` on a non-PWM pin fall back to software PWM; a March 2024 user found `AngularServo` moved "half the expected angle and jitters once it gets there" on a Pi 5 (<https://github.com/gpiozero/gpiozero/discussions/1128>), and a Pi 5 user driving two MG996R servos with gpiozero on GPIO 12 and 13 from a separate 5 V supply reported they "jitter A LOT" (<https://raspberrypi.stackexchange.com/questions/147341/raspberry-pi-5-servo-jitter>). Servo electrical specifications from an Indian seller: SG90 1.2 kg·cm at 4.8 V, 3.0–7.2 V, 9 g; MG90S 1.8 kg·cm at 4.8 V and 2.2 kg·cm at 6 V, 4.8–6.0 V, 13.4 g (<https://robocraze.com/blogs/post/all-about-servo-motor-sg90>). Stall current for either: not found from a source I would trust; it varies by maker and has to be measured.

### DC motors through drivers

DRV8833 (Pololu carrier): two H-bridges, 2.7–10.8 V, 1.2 A continuous and 2 A peak per channel, inputs "3V- and 5V-compatible", under-voltage lockout, over-current and over-temperature protection, reverse-voltage protection, and no separate logic supply needed (<https://www.pololu.com/product/2130>). TB6612FNG (Pololu carrier): recommended motor voltage 4.5–13.5 V, logic 2.7–5.5 V, 1 A continuous and 3 A peak per channel, PWM up to 100 kHz, and an STBY pin that must be driven high (<https://www.pololu.com/product/713>); Both take a PWM signal on their inputs, so either the RP1 hardware PWM channels or lgpio's software PWM can drive them; for a motor that only needs to pull a lining up and stop, PWM timing jitter matters far less than for a servo, since the driver is either on or off over many milliseconds.

### Steppers

A4988 (Pololu carrier): 8–35 V motor supply, logic 3–5.5 V, "up to approximately 1 A per phase without a heat sink or forced air flow" and 2 A with cooling, step-and-direction interface, microstepping down to 1/16, current limit set by a potentiometer (<https://www.pololu.com/product/1182>). The 8 V minimum means an A4988 cannot run from the Pi's own 5 V. TMC2209: not checked in this pass. Its STEP pulse train has the same outlier problem from Linux user space as software PWM (§1).

### Small pumps and heaters

For the ink-pump and heater options, the driver is the same kind of part: a DC pump through one DRV8833 channel, or a heater through a logic-level MOSFET switched by a GPIO or PWM pin. The current a particular small pump or heating element draws was not looked up; it has to be read from its label or measured, and it decides whether the toy's supply can carry it alongside the Pi. For a heater inside fabric, IS 15644 (the electric-toys standard, §7) has a "Heating and abnormal operation" clause (Clause 9) that BIS lists as one of the three electric tests a licensed manufacturer must be able to perform in-house (<https://www.bis.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/10-steps-for-BIS-toy-certification-final2.pdf>). A thermistor read by an ADC (§4) gives the code a temperature to act on; code makes the decision to stop heating, and a pull-down on the MOSFET gate keeps the heater off while the Pi boots or after the program dies (the reset-state point in §1).

### Separate rails, common ground, brownouts

Every source read puts motors and large LED loads on their own supply with the grounds joined (Adafruit, above; the Robocraze servo collection page advises "power decoupling where servo current spikes cause controller resets or jitter", <https://robocraze.com/collections/servo-motors-controllers>). The Pi 5's own budget: it wants 5.1 V at 5 A for full USB current; with any other supply it limits downstream USB to 600 mA (power-supplies documentation, above; details and the `usb_max_current_enable` and `PSU_MAX_CURRENT` overrides are in `research-notes.md` §1). A shared 5 V rail between motors and the Pi shows up on the Pi as under-voltage: `vcgencmd get_throttled` reports it, `vcgencmd pmic_read_adc EXT5V_V` reads the input voltage, and a Geekworm UPS user logging that voltage every second saw minimums of 5.03 V and occasional readings below 4.9 V under stress with the UPS in the path, against a steady 5.12–5.15 V from the official supply directly (<https://geekworm.com/community/forum/topic/144118/x1203-ups-voltage-drops>). No measurement of a hobby motor or servo browning out a Pi 5 on a shared rail was found; that is a bench test.

## 4. Sensors for a toy or small robot

All of the parts below are I2C breakouts with Adafruit CircuitPython drivers that run on the Pi through Blinka; Adafruit maintains a Pi 5 Blinka page (<https://circuitpython.org/blinka/raspberry_pi_5/>), and a user's ADS1115 simple test on a Pi 5 printed readings in an issue thread (<https://github.com/adafruit/Adafruit_CircuitPython_ADS1x15/issues/105>). On Trixie they install with pip inside a virtual environment; no source checked all of them under Trixie specifically.

Thermistor and ADC. The Pi has no analogue input on its header (RP1's 12-bit ADC is internal, §1). A 10 kΩ NTC with B25/50 = 3950, ±1 %, thermal time constant ≤ 15 s, range −55 to 125 °C (Adafruit's epoxy part, <https://www.adafruit.com/product/372>) in a divider needs an external ADC. ADS1115: 16-bit, 8–860 samples per second, four single-ended or two differential inputs, gain up to ×16, 2–5.5 V supply, I2C with four selectable addresses (<https://www.adafruit.com/product/1085>; <https://robocraze.com/products/16-bit-i2c-4-channel-ads1115-module>); library `adafruit-circuitpython-ads1x15`. The alternative is a Pico's own ADC (three channels on a Pico 2, <https://www.raspberrypi.com/products/raspberry-pi-pico-2/>), with the Pico reporting a number to the Pi.

Capacitive touch. MPR121: up to 12 electrodes, I2C address 0x5A to 0x5D (four chips, 48 pads, on one bus), "handles all the filtering for you"; Adafruit suggests copper foil pads (<https://www.adafruit.com/product/1982>). The bare Indian breakout has no regulator and must be fed 2.5–3.6 V, i.e. the Pi's 3.3 V, and has 10 kΩ pull-ups on SDA, SCL and IRQ set by jumpers (<https://robocraze.com/products/mpr121-12-channel-capacitive-touch-sensor-controller-module-i2c-interface>). Library `adafruit-circuitpython-mpr121`. Touch through the fabric and stuffing of a soft toy was not found measured.

IMU for squeeze and shake. The BNO055 and BNO085, which do their own sensor fusion, need I2C clock stretching, which the Pi 5's RP1 controller supports (§1). The one toy paper in §8 that classifies how a toy is handled (Echo, Bartolomei et al. 2025) used an onboard IMU's tilt, angular velocity and acceleration and a model running in the toy's embedded electronics. Which IMU suits a squeeze, as opposed to a shake, was not established; a squeeze may read better on a pressure or force sensor than on an accelerometer.

Time-of-flight distance. VL53L1X: about 30–4,000 mm, up to 50 Hz, narrow cone, 2.8 V chip on a regulated breakout (<https://www.adafruit.com/product/3967>); fixed I2C address 0x29, so more than one needs a multiplexer or the shutdown-pin readdressing trick, about 20 mW at 10 Hz, 940 nm Class 1 emitter (<https://robocraze.com/products/vl53l1x-tof-distance-sensor-breakout-with-4-meter-range-7semi>). Library `adafruit-circuitpython-vl53l1x`.

Cameras. Picamera2 is the Python library for Raspberry Pi cameras and is packaged for Trixie (0.3.38, above); Camera Module 3 and the AI Camera both work through libcamera and `rpicam-apps`. The AI Camera (Sony IMX500) needs `sudo apt install imx500-all`, which installs the sensor firmware, model files in `/usr/share/imx500-models/` and the `rpicam-apps` post-processing stages; "if the neural network model firmware hasn't been previously cached, this might take several minutes" at camera start; MobileNet SSD detection runs on the camera with no significant post-processing on the Pi, while PoseNet output needs host post-processing; Picamera2 has an IMX500 helper class for input and output tensors, with examples in `picamera2-examples/examples/imx500` (<https://github.com/raspberrypi/documentation/blob/master/documentation/asciidoc/accessories/camera/ai-camera.adoc>). Its memory limits and converter are in `research-notes.md` §2.

## 5. The Linux-plus-microcontroller split

### Why Linux is not real-time, in numbers

A preliminary OSPERT 2024 study ran `cyclictest` (4 threads, 100 µs interval, priority 99) for an hour on a Pi 5 under Debian 12 while `stress-ng` ran all its stressors and `iperf3` loaded the network: the stock 6.6.21 kernel had a 14.69 µs mean and a 36,802 µs maximum scheduling latency; the same kernel with PREEMPT_RT had 5.91 µs mean and 124 µs maximum, a 294-fold improvement in the worst case on an untuned kernel (<https://antonio.paolillo.be/publications/workshops/ecrtsOspert2024_dewit_rtlinux_paper.pdf>). A forum user on a 6.13 RT kernel under a `stress` memory load still saw, overnight, "in excess of 1000 us (1 ms)" (<https://forums.raspberrypi.com/viewtopic.php?t=382231>); a 2026 IEEE Access paper on a custom PREEMPT_RT 6.15 kernel reported under 200 µs under heavy load (Choudhry et al., §8). Whether Raspberry Pi ships a ready PREEMPT_RT kernel package for Trixie: not found; the reports above built their own.

For a toy that only needs to change a light or start a motor within a few tens of milliseconds of a decision, these latencies are not the limit; the 2-second audio window and the model's inference time are (`research-notes.md` §5). The latency problem is waveform generation: servo pulses, step pulses and LED bit streams, where one late wake-up is a visible twitch.

### The common pattern

The Pi runs the model and code makes the decision; a microcontroller holds the waveform. Raspberry Pi's own description of the Pico family is "ideal for real-time control and lightweight embedded projects" (<https://www.raspberrypi.com/documentation/computers/raspberry-pi.html>). Pico 2 (RP2350): dual Cortex-M33 or dual Hazard3 RISC-V at 150 MHz, 520 KB SRAM, 4 MB flash, 16 PWM channels, 3 ADC channels, 12 PIO state machines, 2 UART, 2 SPI, 2 I2C, USB 1.1, 1.8–5.5 V input, −20 to +85 °C, production promised until at least January 2040 (<https://www.raspberrypi.com/products/raspberry-pi-pico-2/>). The same pattern appears in recent robot papers: an offline home robot used a Raspberry Pi 3B as "AI brain" and an ESP32 as "real-time body controller" (OMNI, 2026, <https://consensus.app/papers/details/9c57817b3f5d5c97bec7cffd94d6904e/>); a quadruped put independent Pico 2 W boards on each part of the body, running a 1 kHz motor loop on one core under FreeRTOS (Gomes et al., §8).

### Link latency, measured

Over USB CDC serial, a benchmark note from August 2026 reports a host-to-board round trip of 0.26 ms for RP2350 running MicroPython against 0.78 ms for a SAMD21 running CircuitPython, from an aarch64 Linux host, and says the difference is "interpreter cost not wire speed"; its bench setup is behind a sign-in, so the host model and message size are not known (<https://sargineer.com/n/sargbench1/usb-cdc-round-trip-rp2350-micropython-0-26>). Gomes et al. found USB serial the bottleneck for streaming: telemetry capped at about 400 Hz although the Pico 2 W generated over 500 Hz internally (§8). Throughput is not the constraint: MicroPython's USB CDC reached about 9.5 Mbit/s from a Pico after a 2021 fix (<https://github.com/micropython/micropython/issues/7479>), far beyond one label every two seconds; the number that matters is the round trip and its worst case.

### MicroPython or the C SDK on the Pico

MicroPython's bytecode "can be 10-20 times slower than the equivalent C code" (<https://github.com/thinking-binaries/tbl-ftag/blob/main/performance.md>); a GPIO toggle loop measured about 88–120 kHz in MicroPython against an estimated 50 MHz in the C SDK, and 31 MHz from MicroPython with the Viper emitter writing registers directly (<https://qiita.com/teddokano/items/a9e96a6b5c5142640f98>). For actuation the gap mostly does not matter, because the waveform itself comes from the Pico's hardware PWM or PIO, which MicroPython configures and then leaves running; MicroPython's `machine.PWM` and `rp2.PIO` do this. A Raspberry Pi engineer's advice on a MicroPython timing problem was that C gives "more consistent performance, as there's no memory garbage-collection being done in the background" (<https://github.com/micropython/micropython/issues/7479>). The Pico SDK documents `hardware_pwm` and `hardware_pio` for C (<https://www.raspberrypi.com/documentation/pico-sdk/hardware.html>).

### RP1's own PIO

User code can use it, with limits. Through piolib, "most PIOLib operations take at least 10 microseconds", because each is a message to RP1 firmware; programs that set up a state machine and then stream data (WS2812, PWM) work, "but anything that requires close coupling between the state machine and driver software is likely to have difficulties"; interrupts were absent in the first release (<https://www.raspberrypi.com/news/piolib-a-userspace-library-for-pio-control/>). piolib's examples include WS2812, PWM, a quadrature encoder decoder and a state-machine inspector (<https://github.com/raspberrypi/utils/tree/master/piolib>). There are four state machines for everything, so `pwm-pio` channels, an LED chain and an encoder compete for them. A Pico 2 has twelve (above). For a toy with one LED chain, RP1's PIO removes the need for a second board; for a robot with several servos, steppers or encoders, a Pico is the room to grow.

## 6. Robotics software on the Pi 5

### ROS 2

Raspberry Pi OS is not a binary platform for ROS 2. ROS's own Raspberry Pi page: arm64 receives Tier 1 support, but "Raspberry Pi OS is based on Debian which receives Tier 3 support, but it can run Ubuntu docker containers for Tier 1 support"; the two supported routes are 64-bit Ubuntu on the Pi or Raspberry Pi OS with the official `ros:{DISTRO}-ros-core` images in Docker (<https://github.com/ros2/ros2_documentation/blob/kilted/source/How-To-Guides/Installing-on-Raspberry-Pi.rst>). Current distributions: Jazzy Jalisco (LTS, Ubuntu 24.04), Kilted Kaiju (May 2025, non-LTS, 1.5 years) and Lyrical Luth (May 2026, LTS until May 2031) (<https://github.com/ros2/ros2_documentation/blob/kilted/source/The-ROS2-Project/Release-Schedule.rst>; <https://github.com/ros2/ros2_documentation/blob/rolling/source/Releases/Release-Lyrical-Luth.rst>). Lyrical is Tier 1 on Ubuntu 26.04 arm64 and Tier 3 (build from source) on Debian Trixie, which is what Raspberry Pi OS Trixie is based on, with Trixie support ending 9 August 2028 (<https://github.com/ros2/ros2_documentation/blob/rolling/source/Releases/lyrical/supported-platforms.rst>). A Raspberry Pi OS host with ROS 2 in Docker keeps the Pi's own GPIO, camera and library support but needs device pass-through, DDS discovery and permissions configured (<https://rottenwifi.com/dr-footlegs-guide-run-ros-2-on-a-raspberry-pi-5-without-replacing-raspberry-pi-os/>).

At toy scale the cost is mainly setup. A 2026 classroom robot on a Pi 4B switched ROS 2 nodes on and off by mode "to reduce computational load and energy consumption" (Aucapiña et al., §8). A toy with one microphone, one model and one actuator has none of what ROS is for (many processes passing messages, standard drivers for lidars and arms, simulation); a single Python process, with a serial line to a Pico where needed, does the same job. micro-ROS exists for ROS messages on a Pico (Gomes et al., §8). For other people's robots the choice is theirs, and the Pi 5 hosts ROS 2 either in Docker on Raspberry Pi OS or natively on Ubuntu.

### The vision side of the range

On the Pi 5 CPU, Ultralytics' own benchmarks at FP32, 640 × 640, on COCO128: YOLO26n takes 67.03 ms per image in NCNN (about 15 fps, derived), 91.87 ms in MNN, 104.55 ms in OpenVINO, 123.30 ms in LiteRT, 125.99 ms in ONNX and 299.09 ms in PyTorch; YOLO26s takes 172.88 ms in NCNN and 355.69 ms in ONNX; YOLO11n in ONNX took 147.20 ms (Ultralytics 8.4.x; <https://github.com/ultralytics/ultralytics/blob/main/docs/en/guides/raspberry-pi.md>). These are model times per image, not camera pipelines. A paper measuring a real pipeline with YOLOv8 converted to NCNN on a Pi 5 reported 6.58–7.50 fps including camera capture and post-processing (Emmanuel, Lacy and Ismail, 2025, <https://consensus.app/papers/details/6c0ef4ba8a6a5e6782a911bd53e1b6e8/>). Hailo-8L on a Pi 5 with YOLOv8s at 640 × 640, int8, from a 240 fps video file: 41 fps at PCIe Gen 2, 82 fps at Gen 3, against 2 fps on the Pi without it; batch size changed Gen 3 throughput from 80 fps (batch 2) to 120 fps (batch 8), then down to 100 (16) and 54 (32) (Seeed Studio staff on the Raspberry Pi forums, <https://forums.raspberrypi.com/viewtopic.php?t=373669>; <https://forums.raspberrypi.com/viewtopic.php?t=373867>). Hailo's Model Zoo reference figures for YOLOv8s at 640 × 640 are measured on an Intel Core i5 host, not a Pi: Hailo-8L 110 fps at batch 1, Hailo-8 491 fps, Hailo-10H 166 fps (as tabulated with the release numbers at <https://datarootlabs.com/blog/hailo-ai-kit-raspberry-pi-5-setup-and-computer-vision-pipelines>). A 2025 Cal Poly senior project measured whole pipelines: Pi 5 CPU 3.8 inference fps at 7.62 W, AI Camera 9.2 inference fps at 30 camera fps and 6.45 W, Hailo-8L over 30 inference fps at 7.80 W (Babian, <https://digitalcommons.calpoly.edu/eesp/700>; model not named in the abstract). Arducam's IMX500 tables give 30 fps through the MIPI image path for YOLOv8n detection, pose, classification and segmentation and for SSD MobileNet (<https://arducam.github.io/imx500-mcu-sdk/md_docs_2benchmark.html>). Batch-8 throughput is not single-frame response time; for a robot reacting to one frame, the batch-1 or pipeline figures are the relevant ones.

## 7. Toys: the privacy record, the Indian rules, and batteries

### What went wrong with connected toys

The record is consistent: the harm came from voices and data leaving the toy. CloudPets (Spiral Toys) kept 821,296 account records in a MongoDB database with no authentication, indexed by Shodan, and links to 2,182,337 voice recordings in an Amazon S3 bucket that also needed no authentication; the data was copied by several parties and held to ransom, and the exposure ran from 25 December 2016 until access was cut on 13 January 2017 (Troy Hunt, <https://www.troyhunt.com/data-from-connected-cloudpets-teddy-bears-leaked-and-ransomed-exposing-kids-voice-messages/>; SecurityWeek, <https://www.securityweek.com/insecure-cloudpets-database-exposed-credentials-private-data/>; BBC, <https://www.bbc.com/news/technology-39115001>). On 17 February 2017 Germany's Federal Network Agency banned the My Friend Cayla doll and advised parents to destroy it; the researcher who reported it said hackers could use its unsecured Bluetooth "to listen and talk to the child", "even through several walls" (Reuters, <https://www.reuters.com/article/technology/germany-bans-talking-doll-cayla-citing-security-risk-idUSKBN15W20T/>). Researchers interviewing parent-child pairs who played with Hello Barbie and CogniToys Dino found "children were often unaware that others might be able to hear what was said to the toy" (McReynolds et al. 2017, §8). A 2024 black-box study of an AI toy, the Grok toy from Curio Interactive, which its authors describe as integrating ChatGPT (an OpenAI product, reported here as found, not recommended), found it "transmits a continuous data stream to the servers while it is switched on" (Pavliv, Akbari and Wagner, §8). A 2026 Croatian study built a Raspberry Pi toy prototype with speech, audio, camera and network and found authentication, data-protection and unsecured-channel weaknesses "similar to reported incidents involving commercial smart toys" (Rubčić and Grediček, <https://consensus.app/papers/details/06260233cfbe584884df43517d9b4c1d/>).

What on-device processing changes, as the sources frame it: every incident above needed the audio or data to leave the toy, over Bluetooth, Wi-Fi or a cloud store. A toy whose model runs on its own Pi, keeps no recording and has no network enabled has none of those paths. It still has a microphone, and the Pi 5 has Wi-Fi and Bluetooth on board, so the claim "nothing leaves" holds only if the radios are off and nothing is written to the card; those are configuration facts to check on the built toy (for example `rfkill list`, and what the service writes to disk), not properties of the hardware.

### The Indian rules a physical toy meets

The Toys (Quality Control) Order, 2020 (DPIIT, S.O. 853(E), 25 February 2020) applies to "Product or material designed or clearly intended, whether or not exclusively, for use in play by children under 14 years of age", including "after a toy is subjected to reasonably foreseeable conditions of normal use and abuse"; toys must conform to IS 9873 parts 1, 2, 3, 4, 7 and 9 and IS 15644 "Safety of Electric Toys", and bear the Standard Mark under a BIS licence (Scheme-I); the Order excludes goods meant for export (<http://bis.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Toy_QC_order.pdf>). The gazetted Order says it came into force on 1 September 2020; it was extended, and the Government's later statement is that toys came under compulsory BIS certification "with effect from 01/01/2021", applying to domestic manufacturers and to foreign manufacturers exporting to India (PIB, 27 July 2022, <https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1845398&lang=2&reg=48>). BIS's own bulletin: from 1 January 2021 "no person shall manufacture, import, distribute, sell, hire, lease, store or exhibit for sale any toys without ISI mark" (<https://www.bis.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/BIS_Jun_Jul_2020_Issue.pdf>). The Second Amendment Order, 11 December 2020, exempts goods "manufactured and sold by Artisans registered with Office of the Development Commissioner (Handicrafts)" and by registered proprietors and authorised users of a Geographical Indication (<https://www.bis.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Toys-Quality-Control-Second-Amendment-Order-2020.pdf>).

For certification BIS sorts toys into two kinds: non-electric, primary standard IS 9873 (Part 1):2019, and electric, "toys which have at least one function dependent on electricity", primary standard IS 15644:2006, with the other parts tested as secondary standards (<https://bis.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Safety_of_toys.pdf>). IS 15644:2006 is identical to IEC 62115:2003, and IS 9873 Part 1 to ISO 8124-1:2018 (Bureau Veritas bulletin, <https://cdn1-www.cps.bureauveritas.com/sites/g/files/zypfnx236/files/media/document/Bulletin-20B-048.pdf>). An electric-toy licensee must test three of IS 15644's thirteen requirements in-house (Clauses 8, 9 and 10: power input, heating and abnormal operation, electric strength at operating temperature) and may subcontract the rest to BIS-recognised labs (<https://www.bis.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/10-steps-for-BIS-toy-certification-final2.pdf>; <https://www.bis.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/toys-faqs.pdf>). The 2003 IEC edition's battery clauses, and whether BIS has notified a later edition, were not checked in this pass.

What the sources say about a prototype against a sold product, stated as the texts read and not as advice: the Order's obligations attach to toys and to the acts BIS lists (manufacture, import, distribute, sell, hire, lease, store, exhibit for sale), and it applies to products "clearly intended" for play by children under 14. The texts read do not define a prototype, a research object or an artwork, and they say nothing about a single object kept by its maker. Whether a soft toy made as an art or research object, shown or lent, falls under the Order, and whether Hoja is "intended for use in play by children under 14" or by adults, is a question for a lawyer or for BIS, and the answer could change if it is ever sold or given out.

Children's data is a separate law. Section 9 of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 requires a data fiduciary to obtain "verifiable consent of the parent" before processing any personal data of a child, forbids processing "likely to cause any detrimental effect on the well-being of a child", and forbids "tracking or behavioural monitoring of children or targeted advertising directed at children" (<https://indiankanoon.org/doc/98869575/>). The DPDP Rules were notified on 14 November 2025 (MeitY, <https://www.meity.gov.in/documents/act-and-policies/digital-personal-data-protection-rules-2025-gDOxUjMtQWa?pageTitle=Digital-Personal-Data-Protection-Rules-2025.pdf>), and a report on them says the children's-data requirements take effect 18 months from notification (MediaNama, <https://www.medianama.com/2025/11/223-dpdp-rules-real-time-child-tracking-without-consent/>), which derived is about May 2027. Under the Act, "child" means under 18 (same report). Whether a toy that classifies speech on the device and keeps nothing is "processing personal data" within the Act's definitions was not established here.

### Battery-powered Pi 5

The Pi 5's supply rules (5 V 5 A for full USB current, 600 mA otherwise, the EEPROM and `config.txt` overrides) are in `research-notes.md` §1; a staff-level forum answer adds that both overrides "override the PD mode validation and USB port current limiting. It is up to you to ensure the Pi is actually getting 5A" (<https://forums.raspberrypi.com/viewtopic.php?t=374790>). Battery boards that supply 5 V at 5 A include SunFounder's PiPower 5 (two-cell 2,000 mAh 18650 pack, £35/$35; the Raspberry Pi magazine review says "up to around five hours, depending on the workload", load not stated, <https://magazine.raspberrypi.com/articles/pipower-5>), DFRobot's four-cell 18650 UPS HAT ($53, "up to 10 hours" claimed by the seller, <https://www.dfrobot.com/product-2840.html>) and Waveshare's UPS HAT (E), whose documentation warns that at high load and low battery the system may "repeatedly reboot" and ships a low-voltage shutdown script (<https://docs.waveshare.com/UPS-HAT-E-Tutorials-Raspberry-Pi>). A measured runtime of a Pi 5 on a power bank or UPS HAT with the load stated: not found. A benchmark repository exists for measuring it, logging temperature and throttling with `fsync()` until the bank dies (<https://github.com/Lucas-Sperotto/rpi5-powerbank-battery-bench>). The 2026 classroom robot reported 96 minutes in education mode and 75.6 minutes in navigation mode on a Pi 4B (Aucapiña et al., §8; battery capacity not given in the abstract).

Derived, for scale only: a 10,000 mAh power bank's cells hold about 37 Wh at 3.7 V nominal; at an assumed 85 % conversion efficiency about 31 Wh reach the Pi; at a 5 W average draw (between the measured 2.4–3.3 W idle and 8.9–9.8 W stress figures in `research-notes.md` §1) that is about 6 hours, and at 8 W about 4 hours. The real figure depends on his duty cycle, his LEDs or heater, and whether the bank holds 5 V under the Pi's load steps, which several listings warn it may not.

## 10. Open questions for the bench

Only measurement on his own Pi 5 and parts can settle these.

1. Whether his WS2812B ring or strip reads the Pi 5's 3.3 V data line reliably with no shifter, with the BSS138 module, and with a 74AHCT125 if one can be found; check across the whole chain at the brightness levels the darkening uses, with the toy's own supply.

2. Whether the PIO-based NeoPixel library and the SPI method both run on his Trixie image as installed (`/dev/pio0` present, group ownership, firmware version), and what each costs in CPU while the audio model is running.

3. The LED current of his actual parts at each brightness the darkening uses, including fully dark, measured with a meter in series, since two products sold as WS2812B differed by more than half in the PJRC test.

4. Servo pulse jitter on his Pi 5 with the RP1 hardware PWM, with `pwm-pio`, and with gpiozero software PWM, while the AST model runs at full load, captured on a logic analyser; whether the servo twitches visibly in each case.

5. Whether a motor, servo, pump or heater on the same 5 V rail as the Pi causes under-voltage: log `vcgencmd pmic_read_adc EXT5V_V` and `vcgencmd get_throttled` once a second while each actuator starts, stalls and stops, first on a shared supply and then on a separate one with grounds joined.

6. Stall and running current of his SG90 or MG90S and of any pump he chooses, measured, since no trusted source gave them.

7. What every output pin does from power-on through boot, after the program exits, and after it crashes, with the heater MOSFET or motor driver connected; whether the `gpio=` line in `config.txt` plus a gate pull-down keeps every actuator off.

8. Whether the MPR121 detects touch through the toy's fabric and stuffing, at which electrode size and threshold, and whether an IMU or a pressure sensor separates a squeeze from a shake better on his toy.

9. The round-trip latency and its worst case between his Pi 5 and a Pico 2 over USB serial and over UART0 (`dtparam=uart0`, `/dev/ttyAMA0`), MicroPython and C, under full model load; the only published round-trip figure (0.26 ms) has no visible setup.

10. Runtime of the whole toy on the power bank or UPS board he would actually use, at his duty cycle, logged to the end with the battery-bench script or an equivalent, and whether the bank holds 5 V at the Pi's load steps.

11. With Wi-Fi and Bluetooth off and the service running, that nothing is transmitted and nothing is written: `rfkill list`, an outgoing-traffic check, and a listing of what changes on the card over an hour of use.

12. Whether Hoja, as he intends to make and show it, is a "toy" under the Toys (Quality Control) Order and whether DPDP section 9 reaches on-device classification with nothing stored; these are questions for a lawyer or BIS, not for the bench, and are listed so they are not lost.
